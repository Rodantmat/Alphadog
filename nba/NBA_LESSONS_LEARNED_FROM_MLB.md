# NBA Project — Lessons Learned From MLB (Statistical Methodology + Pitfalls)

*This is the single most important document in this transfer package. It captures a research standard built the hard way, across dozens of strategy candidates, almost all of which looked real at first and were later found to be artifacts. Apply every one of these checks to NBA from day one — do not rediscover them by repeating the same mistakes.*

---

## Part A — The complete research standard (apply to every NBA strategy candidate)

This standard was built incrementally, each item added after a real mistake exposed the gap it now closes. Apply all of them together, every time, not selectively.

### 1. The fair-odds gate (apply FIRST, before any hit-rate analysis)
Before trusting any high hit-rate finding, compute the implied house edge: `p × m` where `p` is the real hit rate and `m` is the real per-leg multiplier. If this implies the platform is handing out a large, systematic edge on a repeatable, high-volume line, **the multiplier attribution is wrong, not the market** — go find the real multiplier before believing the hit rate. State the platform's realistic edge corridor explicitly and check every multiplier against it as a mechanical, first-pass filter (MLB's corridors: Goblin-style tiered pricing 2-10%, Demon-style high-risk pricing 20-35%, Standard/fixed-table pricing 5-15%, per-leg dynamic pricing books 8-26% depending on platform). **This single check resolved 5 of MLB's ~13 rejected strategies faster than any hit-rate analysis could.**

### 2. Never apply a tier/pool-level multiplier to a heterogeneous population
If a "tier" or pool is actually multiple sub-cells with different true probabilities, compute `Σ wᵢ(pᵢ·mᵢ)` weighted by real volume per cell — never a single blended rate. MLB's costliest single error was exactly this: an aggregate hit rate driven by ultra-safe cells was paired with a multiplier from a different, small-volume cell, producing a phantom positive edge that took a full Gemini adversarial pass to catch.

### 3. Multiple, genuinely different research passes per candidate — not the same query with a different threshold
For each candidate signal, test: raw historical hit rate vs. the model's own probability estimate; player-level vs. prop-line-level pooling; single-factor vs. multi-layered combinations; and any domain-specific interaction (e.g., MLB tested lineup-slot interaction because a real, documented gradient existed on one side of the market — check for an NBA equivalent, e.g., minutes/role tier interaction).

### 4. Gemini as a genuine adversary, not a rubber stamp — used correctly
Gemini should be asked to **set its own falsification bar before seeing the result** (minimum sample size, required monotonicity, minimum edge over baseline), then the actual number is checked against that pre-stated bar. Work in **multiple small passes**, not one large dump — MLB's explicit, repeated lesson was that large single prompts caused drift; the working pattern is: present data + sharp questions → get a diagnosis → reference that diagnosis explicitly in a follow-up pass for the next specific piece, without re-pasting everything.

**Gemini is valuable but not infallible in either direction** — MLB found real cases of Gemini correctly catching an analyst's own contamination the analyst missed, and separate real cases of Gemini being wrong about a proposed mechanism (confidently predicting a mechanism that a direct test then refuted) and about factual claims (incorrectly asserting historical data could resolve something that turned out to not exist at all when checked directly). Test Gemini's claims and mechanisms the same way you test your own — don't accept or reject on authority.

### 5. The three-check discipline on every number before reporting it
(a) Correct lane/join — confirm you're reading the right variant/tier/category via a validated join, not a raw flag that may be stale or wrong. (b) Corrupted/known-bad day exclusion — check for and exclude any day with a confirmed data-quality issue. (c) Day-robustness / leave-one-day-out — a pooled, aggregate result can be entirely carried by one or two outlier days; always break down by day and check the sign holds broadly, not just in aggregate.

### 6. Statistical significance done properly — day-level clustering, not pooled-leg-level
**This is one of the single highest-value lessons from the entire MLB research program.** Treating same-day legs as independent observations when computing standard error inflates apparent significance by 3-5x, because legs within a day are correlated (a strong slate lifts everything together). MLB found multiple cases of a pooled Z-score of 3-5+ collapsing to a clustered t-statistic under 1.5 once properly clustered by day. **Always compute significance at the day level** (treat each day as one observation, N = number of days, not number of legs), not the pooled-leg level.

### 7. Multiple-comparisons correction, scaled to what was actually searched
When scanning many cells/props/thresholds for the best-looking result, the significance bar must scale with how many things were searched (Bonferroni or equivalent). A single, pre-registered confirmatory test on one specific cell should use an *uncorrected* bar — using a scan-level bar on a single confirmatory test is itself an error (MLB made and caught this mistake once: it wrongly used a 40-cell-scan-corrected bar on what was actually a single pre-specified test, making a real, borderline-positive result look far more rejected than the evidence warranted).

### 8. "Insufficient data / underpowered" is a different, distinct verdict from "confirmed negative" — don't collapse them
A non-significant result with a wide confidence interval that still contains a materially positive value is NOT the same as a confirmed-zero effect. State the actual power calculation (how many days would be needed to detect the effect size in question) and track genuinely underpowered candidates in their own list with an explicit unblock condition (e.g., "re-check once N ≥ X days of data exist"), rather than either reporting them as dead or leaving them ambiguous.

### 9. Check your OWN statistical treatment for bias in both directions, not just for being "too strict"
MLB found and named a specific, subtle failure mode: introducing a stricter statistical test **specifically because** a result survived further than expected (moving the goalposts only when something looks promising) is a real form of bias, distinct from and just as important to avoid as being too lenient on a promising-looking result. When you find yourself reaching for a new, more rigorous check, ask whether that same check was applied to everything that came before it too. Separately: the very rare case of an error that pushes a result too *conservative* (found once in ~7 documented benchmark errors on the whole MLB program, versus 6 that inflated) still matters — check for it explicitly, don't assume all bias runs one direction.

### 10. Enumerate every possible data source before declaring something "untestable" or "no real data exists"
**This is the second-highest-value lesson from the whole program.** MLB repeatedly assumed a question was unanswerable from real data, then later discovered hundreds to tens of thousands of relevant real rows sitting in a table nobody had checked, because the search stopped at the first or most obvious table. Before concluding "untestable," systematically list every schema/table that could plausibly hold the answer (check what code actually writes where, including tables that a given pipeline stage explicitly *skips* — that's often exactly where undiscovered real data hides) — don't stop at the first negative result.

### 11. A plausible causal story is not evidence — test it directly, including your own
MLB's "mechanistically coherent" trap: a prop's apparent correlation strength tracked a real, physical-sounding narrative (a composite stat should correlate more with game environment) that turned out to be a pure artifact of a different confound (line-threshold variance) once tested properly. The physics-plausible story should have raised suspicion, not confidence, once an independent check (does this ordering make physical sense given what components are shared across props) was available. Test your own favored explanation with the same rigor you'd apply to someone else's.

### 12. Same-game correlation is real but usually smaller than DFS-community folklore suggests, and platforms actively price against it
External research (published DFS/sharp-bettor material) correctly predicted that pick'em platforms price legs as independent even though same-game props are genuinely correlated — but MLB's own measurement found the raw effect was **inflated by multiple contamination sources** (pooling across props with different base rates, unweighted game-size averaging, nested/nearly-deterministic same-player multi-line stacking) and the clean, corrected effect was much smaller than the initial headline number. Separately and decisively: **when tested with a real live-board same-game vs. cross-game slip comparison, the platform showed a real, large payout discount for same-game stacking** (a ~35-40% lower multiplier for the identical legs), which more than offset the real (much smaller, ~8%, itself not statistically confirmed) correlation benefit. **Do not assume same-game stacking is a working strategy for NBA without directly testing the platform's own same-game discount, the same way — it may be actively priced against, not free money.**

### 13. Platforms price probability directionally but not proportionally — a real, load-bearing finding, but exploiting it requires solving player-level selection, which MLB never managed
Sportsbook-vs-DFS-platform comparison confirmed the Goblin/tiered-pricing mechanism moves its payout only a small fraction of the amount that a linear/proportional pricing model would require for a given probability gap (in MLB's case, roughly a 15% multiplier change for a ~2.6x true-probability gap). This means there IS a real, structural mispricing — but MLB never found a way to *identify in advance* which specific legs sit on the high-probability side of that gap; every walk-forward selection attempt (raw trailing hit rate, model-probability quintiles, appearance frequency) regressed to the pool average. **If NBA replicates this finding, treat "the mechanism is real" and "we can exploit it" as two completely separate, both-unsolved questions** — MLB solved neither the second one.

### 14. Prop-definition mismatches across platforms are a real, repeated trap when comparing cross-app data
The same-sounding prop name can mean genuinely different underlying stats on different platforms (MLB's specific case: a fantasy-score-style composite prop used different scoring formulas on different platforms, producing a large phantom "line difference" that looked like a line-shopping opportunity but was actually just two platforms measuring different things with the same name). **Before any cross-platform prop comparison for NBA, explicitly verify the prop's exact definition/formula on each platform** — don't assume identical names mean identical stats, especially for any composite/fantasy-style prop.

### 15. Before trusting any grouping key or join in a new table, sanity-check that it actually isolates what it claims to
The dominant single failure mode across MLB's entire research program (at least six distinct, separately-discovered instances) was a grouping key or join that failed to isolate the specific unit being measured — instead silently pooling in something else (an opponent's data via an unfiltered join, a different tier/variant sharing a name, multiple simultaneous line-ladder rungs mistaken for time-series movement, etc.). **Before running any substantive analysis on a new table or join for NBA, run these checks first**: does the row count match a sane expectation (an unexpected exact 2x or similar multiple is a strong tell of a fan-out bug)? Is the value distribution/magnitude physically plausible for what it claims to represent? Does a known-null or placebo case behave as expected? Does a same-value control group show exactly zero difference (if it should)? **An implausible result is far more often a broken key than a real discovery — check the key before believing the finding.**

### 16. Real backtest results, however rigorous, still need real-money confirmation before being trusted
Multiple MLB findings survived every backtest check (day-robustness, significance, adversarial review) and were still overturned the moment a real placed slip's actual multiplier was checked against the assumed one. **Whenever a candidate's viability depends on a specific multiplier number, get that number from a real, current slip-builder quote (which costs nothing — the payout displays before placing) as the final, decisive step** — don't deploy real money on an assumed or extrapolated multiplier.

## Part B — Platform-specific pricing mechanics found this session (likely to transfer conceptually to NBA, same platforms)

These are mechanics of the **platforms themselves** (PrizePicks, Underdog, Sleeper), not MLB-specific facts — worth re-verifying on NBA data but starting from these as informed priors rather than testing blind:

- **PrizePicks Goblin/Demon-style tiered pricing**: pricing is discrete/step-function (fixed multiplier per tier), not continuous per-leg pricing. As tier distance from the "anchor" line increases, the easier direction (Goblin-style) pays progressively LESS, while the harder direction (Demon-style) pays progressively MORE, roughly geometrically (~1.4x growth factor per tier step in MLB's case, likely different but directionally similar for NBA).
- **PrizePicks Flex payout tables can have a genuinely different effective probability structure than Power** for the same legs — a mechanism exists ("Single-Tier Non-Arbitrage Principle" — check that no single partial-hit payout tier alone implies positive EV even discarding every other outcome, since that's a mathematical impossibility for a properly-priced table) for detecting corrupted/misremembered Flex tables. Flex partial-credit structure can theoretically make an EV-negative Power pool EV-positive if the underlying leg probabilities are far from what the Flex table's insurance tiers were calibrated for — this was found true in principle for MLB but the real magnitude, when tested, still fell short.
- **Underdog and Sleeper price per-leg dynamically** (closer to real sportsbook-style pricing) rather than off one flat published table — a flat assumed multiplier (e.g., "2-pick always pays 3.5x") is reliably wrong once legs are meaningfully far from a 50/50 probability; real per-leg pricing compresses toward fair odds much more than a flat-table assumption predicts, especially for heavy favorites.
- **Underdog/Sleeper's own EV-parity pricing, when measured directly against real placed-slip data at scale (14,000+ real legs), showed p×m flat and slightly below 1.0 across the entire probability range** — i.e., these platforms price efficiently enough that no simple probability-based leg selection beat the pool average. Treat this as the default hypothesis for NBA on these two platforms until real data says otherwise.
- **PrizePicks discounts same-game correlation meaningfully** (confirmed via a real, direct same-game-vs-cross-game live slip comparison) — always build cross-game unless a same-game correlation strategy has been specifically, directly tested and found to survive this discount.
- **DFS platforms generally do not publish their own per-leg multiplier via API** — expect to build a manual, ongoing multiplier-observation study (real placed slips, recorded and tabulated) as a first-class, permanent research artifact, the same way MLB built its Excel/spreadsheet-style player/prop hit-rate-and-multiplier matrix. Budget for this as ongoing work, not a one-time task.

## Part C — Pipeline/data-quality bug family to actively guard against in NBA from day one

All of the following were real, separately-discovered bugs in MLB, and every one of them is the *same underlying shape*: a query, join, or grouping key that silently included the wrong population.

1. A join on a shared key without a fully-specifying condition (e.g., team+game without player) can fan out and double- or multi-count rows — the tell is an unexpected exact multiple in row counts (2x, 3x) versus the expected population size.
2. A "baseline" or "control" computation that already conditions on the very thing being measured erases the effect it's supposed to measure — always check that a comparison/control group is defined independently of the effect under test.
3. Pooling across sub-groups with different true base rates before computing a ratio/percentage (Jensen-style aggregation bias) can inflate or invent an effect that isn't really there at the pooled level.
4. Nested/hierarchical outcomes (e.g., a lower threshold being automatically implied by a higher one on the same underlying stat) look like independent correlated events but are actually near-deterministic — exclude same-entity nested lines from any "independence/correlation" study.
5. A composite/derived stat computed with two different underlying formulas across data sources produces phantom "differences" that look like real signal.
6. Multiple simultaneous price/line variants (e.g., a full ladder of tiers offered at once) can be mistaken for a time-series of the same thing moving, if the grouping key doesn't also key on the specific variant/tier.

**The single most valuable standing habit from all of this**: whenever an early result looks unexpectedly strong or an aggregate number looks structurally odd, decompose it by every plausible confounding dimension (day, prop, tier, variant, player) before believing it — this exact discipline caught the majority of MLB's false positives before they were reported or acted on.

## Part E — A formal, stricter verification standard found in MLB's own internal work log, worth adopting as NBA's default bar

MLB's own internal, continuously-updated work log documents an explicit, formal rule that is stricter than anything captured elsewhere in this package, and worth adopting directly as NBA's own standing verification bar: **before considering any system deeply verified, require a set number (MLB used two) of genuinely consecutive clean investigation passes — each using different real samples and different angles — with zero new issues found. If any pass turns up something new, no matter how minor, the counter resets to zero, regardless of how many clean passes preceded it.** This is a materially stricter standard than "run one thorough check and move on," and it directly produced real value in MLB's own history — a real scrutiny effort that would have stopped after an early clean-seeming pass instead kept finding genuinely new, real issues across 13 total passes before finally reaching two consecutive clean ones. Apply the same discipline to any NBA system component receiving a dedicated verification effort (the scoring engine, the outcome grader, a new enrichment factor) rather than treating a single clean-looking check as sufficient.

**Several additional, concrete real bug patterns from that same log, each worth actively checking for in NBA's own build:**

- **A "still needs processing" filter that doesn't exclude rows which can structurally never satisfy the condition being waited on causes a genuine infinite loop, not slow progress.** MLB found a real case of a scoring query filtering only on "score is still null," without also excluding rows that could never receive a score because a hard prerequisite value was itself missing — the pipeline endlessly re-attempted the same unscoreable rows forever, and the apparent "progress" (a slowly ticking percentage) was actually stuck, not advancing. **Any NBA processing loop with a "find rows still needing work" filter must also explicitly exclude rows that can never satisfy that condition, or verify total addressable count is actually shrinking over time, not just that some percentage metric is moving.**
- **A generic payload-truncation utility that does a naive byte/character slice on serialized structured data (JSON) can corrupt that data by cutting it mid-field**, producing invalid, garbled output rather than cleanly dropping whole fields. MLB found and traced a real, subtly-caused downstream data-quality bug all the way back to exactly this. **Any NBA utility that truncates a structured payload to fit a size limit must be structure-aware (truncate whole fields/objects, never a raw string slice) — a naive slice is a real, hard-to-trace corruption source.**
- **A read-side filter that silently excludes rows with a missing/null field can hide the very evidence needed to diagnose the upstream bug causing that field to be null in the first place.** MLB found a case where a downstream query required a specific field to be non-null as a basic sanity filter — which had the side effect of quietly excluding exactly the rows suffering from an upstream bug that left that field null, making the bug's true scope look smaller than it really was. **Be suspicious of any filter that excludes rows based on a field being missing — check whether that missingness is itself a symptom worth investigating rather than a legitimate reason to filter the row out silently.**
- **A single real-world entity can appear more than once in a raw upstream feed under genuinely different internal IDs, even when everything else about it (player, prop, line, source, game) is identical** — a real, provider-side quirk MLB found and confirmed via direct row inspection, not assumed to be its own bug. Any NBA deduplication logic should account for this possibility (dedupe on the full set of real, meaningful identity fields, not just trust that an upstream provider's own row ID is always unique for a given real-world offer).
- **Before treating a surprising classification or tier assignment as a bug, check whether a separate, deliberate secondary gate is responsible**, not just the obvious primary threshold. MLB found a real case where legs above the expected primary-tier probability cutoff were still correctly placed in a lower-confidence tier, because a second, independent confidence threshold also had to be met — confirmed by reading the actual code rather than assuming from the surprising outcome alone.
- **When resuming interrupted work, verify the actual current code/data state directly (e.g., via real commit history or a live database query) rather than trusting either party's own memory or report of where things left off.** MLB found a real case where a session resuming after a disconnect found genuine, real commits it had no memory of making — reality had moved since the last known-good checkpoint, and trusting a screenshot or a prior summary instead of checking directly would have produced a wrong picture of current state.

**Reinforcing a pattern already recommended in the architecture document**: MLB's own internal work log operates under a standing, explicit rule — check it first at the start of every new work session, and update it every single time any job is started, fixed, or completed, no matter how small. This is the same discipline behind maintaining a continuous project log for NBA rather than reconstructing status from memory each time — treat it as confirmed, real-world-proven practice, not just a suggested nicety.

## Part F — The exact same rigor discipline, independently required and applied on the scoring/calibration side, not just the strategy-research side

A separate, real MLB investigation into whether the scoring engine's enrichment factors were helping or hurting its baseline model ran into the identical class of problems documented throughout this package's Parts A-E — and the single most important thing it found deserves to be read as a direct, cautionary parallel: **an entire multi-day investigation's founding premise turned out to rest on a lookahead-bias bug in the baseline measurement itself, not in the thing actually being evaluated.**

**The baseline-leakage discovery, in full, because the specifics matter.** The investigation began from a real, seemingly solid finding: the baseline model's own discrimination looked strong (+39.76 percentage points within-cell) while the live, fully-enriched model looked much weaker (+5.31pp) — a huge apparent gap, strongly suggesting the enrichment layer was actively destroying a good baseline. Days of real factor-by-factor diagnostic work followed. Then, an independent, parallel investigation thread discovered that the "baseline as of date D" calculation genuinely included day D's own game results — a real lookahead bug, not a subtle statistical artifact. Once corrected to properly use only data available *before* the prediction date, the "clean" baseline discrimination came out to +5.32pp — statistically indistinguishable from the live enriched model's +5.31pp. **The entire premise was wrong: it was never "enrichment destroys a strong baseline." Both the baseline and the enrichment layer perform almost identically, and both are genuinely modest.** The days of factor-level diagnostic work weren't wasted — real bugs and data gaps were found and remain valid — but the *framing* the whole investigation was measured against never actually existed. **The generalizable, urgent lesson for NBA: before investigating why a component seems to underperform a supposedly-strong reference point, verify the reference point itself as rigorously as the thing being blamed — a "before" or "control" measurement is just as capable of containing a lookahead-bias or leakage bug as the "after" measurement everyone's default instinct is to scrutinize.** This is precisely the same failure family documented in Part A item 11 (a plausible causal story is not evidence) and Part C (grouping-key contamination), but applied to a baseline/reference measurement rather than a candidate finding — check the control group with the same suspicion as the treatment.

**A second, subtler trap found immediately after fixing the first one, worth its own explicit rule: statistical rigor cannot rescue a fundamentally circular evaluation.** Once the leakage was fixed, the same investigation asked whether a specific enrichment factor genuinely improved on the corrected baseline — and caught, before publishing a result, that the factor being tested had originally been *selected* using data from largely the same time window now being used to *evaluate* it. Consulting an adversarial second opinion on this specific concern (the same Gemini-as-adversary pattern from Part A item 4) produced a decisive, important verdict: applying a more sophisticated statistical technique (a day-level block bootstrap) to this situation does not fix the problem — it only tightens a confidence interval around an estimate that is biased at its core. **The correct fix is a genuine, non-overlapping holdout window, not a fancier statistical treatment of contaminated data.** The clever, reusable technique that resolved it: the factor's own original *selection date* naturally provided an honest holdout boundary — data from after that date literally wasn't available when the original selection decision was made, making it a real, non-artificial out-of-sample window rather than a synthetic split invented after the fact. **For NBA: whenever a candidate signal was originally identified using a specific dataset, and it's later unclear whether an evaluation of that signal overlaps with the data used to find it, look for the signal's own real discovery date as a natural, honest holdout boundary** — it's often sitting right there, more defensible than any post-hoc split you'd otherwise have to justify.

**A third, genuinely subtle methodological distinction worth internalizing directly: "does this factor carry information the baseline lacks" and "does adding this factor actually improve the baseline's own accuracy" are different claims, and a factor can pass the first while failing the second.** The same investigation found a factor that had previously been validated by testing whether its own contribution correlated with the *residual error* left over after the baseline's prediction (a real, legitimate test — it answers "does this carry information baseline doesn't already have") — but when the stricter, more directly relevant test was run (does *adding* this factor to the baseline actually improve the combined model's own correlation with real outcomes), the same factor showed no improvement, and even trended slightly negative. **Both tests were run correctly and neither is wrong on its own terms — they simply answer different questions, and only the second one answers what actually matters for whether to deploy a factor.** Before treating any NBA enrichment factor as validated, be explicit about which of these two questions was actually tested, and make sure it's the second one before trusting a "this factor helps" conclusion.

**A fourth, now twice-confirmed lesson worth stating as a standing, mandatory check**: verify that a backtest or analysis is actually evaluating the *current* live coefficient or configuration value, not a value that has since been changed or corrected in the live system. The same MLB investigation found this exact mistake twice in one session — once when a historical bug had already been fixed in the live enrichment code before the investigation started (making a "large finding" actually a description of already-resolved history), and again when a backtest's assumed factor coefficient turned out to be mathematically incompatible with what the coefficient could ever have actually produced once the *current* live value was checked directly. **Before trusting any backtest's conclusion about a specific factor or coefficient, pull the actual current live value directly and confirm the backtest's assumption matches it — do this as a first, cheap, mandatory step, not an afterthought once a result already looks surprising.**

**A fifth, concrete design lesson**: a shared "opponent" or "matchup" concept in a factor's logic can mean genuinely different things depending on which side of a given prop it's being applied to, and reusing the same lookup blindly can encode a real conceptual error. MLB found a real case where an "opposing defense" factor was correctly wired for hitter props (the batter's opponent is the fielding team behind the pitcher he's facing) but silently wrong for a specific pitcher prop, where the pitcher's own team's defense — not the batting team's — is what actually matters for how many hits that pitcher allows. **When any NBA enrichment factor's logic involves an "opponent" or "matchup" lookup and applies to props on both sides of a game (offense-side and defense-side props, if NBA's design ever has both), verify the lookup direction is conceptually correct for each specific prop it's applied to, not assumed uniform.**

**Finally, a real, practical technique worth adopting directly**: the discovery of the baseline-leakage bug came from an independent, parallel investigation thread cross-checking a specific open question against this one's own findings — not from either thread working in isolation. **When a finding is foundational enough that being wrong about it would invalidate a large amount of downstream work (a baseline reference value, a core formula, a key statistical assumption), it's worth deliberately seeking an independent, parallel check on it — a second thread or session working the same question from a different angle — rather than relying on one continuous line of investigation to catch its own foundational errors.**

**A closing, formal discipline from the same investigation's final, decisive test, worth adopting as NBA's standard for any candidate signal's last, deciding check: pre-register the exact test before running it.** Once the leakage and circularity issues above were both fixed, the investigation's final test of a remaining candidate factor was explicitly pre-registered — the exact factor, its sign, the exact holdout date window, the exact statistical bar, and a stated commitment to accept the one-shot result with no further tuning or re-testing — all written down *before* the test was run, not after seeing an appealing or disappointing result. **This is the single strongest available defense against unconscious post-hoc rationalization, and it's cheap to do**: write the complete test specification, commit to accepting whatever it produces, then run it exactly once. Build this into NBA's own final-decision methodology for any candidate signal, not just as an occasional extra step.

**Two things that same pre-registered test did right, worth calling out explicitly:**
- **When the test passed under one legitimate statistical reading but would have failed under a different, also-legitimate reading of the same data, both readings were reported honestly** rather than presenting only the favorable one. Whenever a finding's status depends on which of several legitimate statistical framings is used, report all of them and let the ambiguity stand rather than picking whichever framing makes the strongest case.
- **The multiple-comparisons correction was applied at the selection stage, not just the final confirmatory test.** Before even choosing which single factor to pre-register and test, the investigation computed how many of the ~15 candidates being screened would be expected to look this extreme purely by chance — and found the honest answer was "roughly two would look this significant with no real effect at all," meaning even identifying "the most promising-looking candidate" from a pool needs the same statistical skepticism as testing it once selected. **Apply this same discipline to NBA's own candidate screening: when picking "the best-looking factor" out of many candidates to investigate further, explicitly account for how many would look that good by pure chance given how many were screened — the selection process itself is not exempt from the winner's-curse risk documented throughout Part A.**

**Two concrete, technical database lessons worth carrying into NBA's own analysis tooling**: when a table meant to hold one row per logical key instead contains duplicate keys with genuinely conflicting values, resolve by averaging the conflicting values rather than arbitrarily picking one, unless there's a specific reason to prefer a particular row. And for complex, multi-stage analytical queries against a Postgres/Hyperdrive-style connection, materializing each stage as its own separate, row-count-verified table is more reliable than nesting everything into one large multi-CTE query — a real, repeated finding was that large combined queries against this kind of connection could report success falsely, making per-stage verification (checking the actual row count after each step, not just trusting a success response) a necessary habit, not excess caution.

**A final, structural documentation lesson worth adopting directly for NBA's own reference documents**: when a foundational premise in an existing document is later found to be wrong, put the correction prominently at the very top of the document, stated plainly, before anything else — not buried partway through or left for a reader to discover on their own by cross-referencing a separate log. The same investigation's final dossier opens with an explicit "critical superseding finding, read this first" banner directly contradicting its own original framing, rather than letting a now-outdated premise stand unchallenged for anyone who doesn't read all the way through. Apply the same standard to any NBA reference document that turns out to need a foundational correction.

## Part D — The foundational selection methodology (Rules B0-B0c) — read this before any other strategy work

This is MLB's own original, foundational rule set — everything else in this document is downstream of it. Apply these to NBA from the very first candidate, not as a later refinement.

### Rule B0 — build real graded-outcome buckets; never rank by the platform's own displayed score
The entire selection logic must be: build a real, historical, per-(prop, side, line-or-tier) hit-rate table from **actual graded outcomes**, then select from buckets that clear a real sample-size and hit-rate bar (MLB's original bar: n≥30 real observations, ≥80% hit rate) — independent of whatever the platform's own internal displayed probability/confidence score says. MLB found a real, costly instance of its own prior work ranking legs by the platform's internal score instead of real graded buckets, and re-doing it properly surfaced real, priced-positive buckets that had been missed as a direct result. **Never trust a platform's own confidence/probability display as a substitute for your own real graded-outcome analysis.**

### Rule B0a — every proposed pool must state both its prop CLASS and its pricing LANE explicitly
Two genuinely separate axes matter for any prop: (1) its **class** — does it have a real ladder of multiple simultaneous lines (call these "tiered" props) or only one sensible threshold (call these "fixed" props)? — and (2) its **lane** — which specific pricing tier/variant is it actually being offered in (MLB's case: Standard vs. Goblin vs. Demon)? **Class and lane are independent, and lane is usually the dominant driver of real EV, more than class.** MLB found a single real, exact example — the identical leg, identical ~85% hit rate — pricing at roughly +1300% in one lane and roughly −13% in another, a swing of over 1,300 percentage points from lane alone, with the underlying prop's class (fixed vs. tiered) and hit rate completely unchanged. **Never report a hit rate or a strategy finding without explicitly stating which lane it was measured in — a "high hit rate" claim with no lane specified is not yet a real finding.**

### A dramatic, cautionary real example behind Rule B0a — treat any default/null category value with real suspicion
MLB's own most costly single analytical error came from exactly this axis: a data-writer left a categorical "lane" field unpopulated (defaulting to a 0/0 pattern) on a large fraction of rows, and an earlier analysis pass **interpreted that default/unpopulated value as meaning "the neutral/standard lane"** rather than recognizing it as simply missing data. When finally checked against an independently, reliably-labeled source, over 90% of those "standard-lane" rows turned out to actually be Goblin or Demon lane, just unlabeled — and the resulting phantom "Standard lane pays +1300% more than Goblin lane for the same legs" conclusion was actually "the same legs, duplicated across two lane labels, one of which was simply wrong." **The generalizable lesson**: whenever a categorical field has a default, null, or zero-value state, never assume that default value corresponds to a meaningful category (like "neither variant" or "standard") without independently verifying it against a separately, reliably-labeled source — this is a more dramatic, higher-stakes version of the "grouping key doesn't isolate what it claims" bug family (Part C above), and it should raise the same immediate suspicion.

### Rule B0b — multi-prop pools must be priced per-leg by each leg's own real rate, never a single blended/averaged rate
A real, quantified MLB example: a reported pool combining five different props at one assumed blended multiplier showed +216.5% ROI; the real per-leg rates for those same five props actually spanned an 82% range (roughly 1.15x to 2.09x), and re-pricing the identical pool with each leg's own real rate brought the honest result down to +32.9% — a ~6.5x overstatement from blended pricing alone, not fabrication, just a wrong assumption. **Any pool spanning more than one prop/side/tier bucket must price each leg independently from its own real, current rate. If the real spread between the cheapest and most expensive leg-type in a pool exceeds roughly 20%, state explicitly that a blended/averaged price would be materially wrong.**

### Rule B0c — a ranked-greedy backtest result must be tested against multiple tie-break orders before being trusted
When legs are selected by ranking within a bucket and many legs share the same or very similar scores, the specific tie-break rule (which of several equally-ranked legs gets picked) can by itself swing a backtest's reported ROI dramatically — MLB found a real case of the same configuration swinging from +25.9% to +12.5% purely from tie-break order, with 69% of legs tied within a day on the ranking field used. **Any backtest built by ranking legs within a bucket must be re-run with at least one alternate, equally-legitimate deterministic tie-break order (e.g., an ID sorted ascending vs. descending) and the range reported — a result that isn't stable across tie-break order is not a real finding yet, it's noise dressed as a result.** This is a distinct, additional check beyond day-robustness (Part A, item 5) — it can fail even on a single, otherwise well-behaved day.

### Historical data-quality date cutoffs are real and specific — track them explicitly, don't assume uniform data quality across your whole history
MLB's tier/lane classification logic was itself wrong for real, identifiable stretches of time, fixed on specific dates (several distinct fixes across roughly a two-week span in MLB's case, each correcting a different, real mislabeling mechanism). Any backtest spanning a date range that crosses one of these fix dates needs to either exclude the pre-fix period for the specific analysis the fix affects, or explicitly flag that the pre-fix data may be systematically wrong in a documented way. **For NBA: the moment any classification, tagging, or tier-derivation logic is fixed, write down the exact date, and maintain that list as a first-class artifact — any future backtest crossing one of these dates needs to account for it, not silently pool pre- and post-fix data together.**

### A related trap: a live system's query-time reconstruction logic may not match what a raw, stored column shows
MLB's live deployed system reconstructs certain classification values (e.g., tier) on the fly at query time via a fallback formula, specifically because the raw, stored column is known to be sparsely populated (in one real case, only ~10% populated) — but the live system's real, effective sample is much larger than the raw column alone suggests, because of this reconstruction. **Before concluding a category is thin or unreliable based on a raw stored column, check whether the live system applies query-time reconstruction logic that the historical/backtest analysis needs to replicate** — measuring against the raw, unreconstructed column can produce a dramatically smaller and differently-biased sample than what the live system actually uses in production.

### A concrete, well-documented real NBA-transferable structural pattern worth testing for directly
MLB found that a prop's real hit rate climbs meaningfully and repeatably as tier/ladder-depth increases (the farther a line sits from its real anchor, the safer the "easy-direction" bet becomes) — and the genuinely usable sweet spot was not the theoretical deepest tier (almost always a thin, one-off, unreliable sample) but **the deepest tier that still carries real volume** (MLB's rule of thumb: n≥10-20 real observations). If NBA's platforms offer an equivalent tiered-line ladder mechanism (very likely, since this is a platform-level mechanic), test for this same volume-vs-depth tradeoff directly rather than assuming either extreme (shallowest-safest or deepest-theoretically-best) is the right place to look.
